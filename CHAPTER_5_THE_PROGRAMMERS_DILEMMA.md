# CHAPTER 5: The Programmer's Dilemma
## *The Morality of Profit*

---

## NIGHT JOURNAL - Wednesday Morning, 6:15 AM
### Warrenton Workshop (Final Week)
### By Moteka Tlhoro

*Written surrounded by boxes. Packing. Leaving. The garage workshop that protected me for three years is becoming a memory. I thought I'd feel sad. Instead, I feel ready.*

---

**On teaching what you're still learning:**

Yesterday, I got an email from a developer in Soweto. Thabo Ndlovu. Twenty-four years old. Built an inventory management system for small retailers. Brilliant work. Charging R500/month.

The system is worth R5,000/month minimum.

He's undercharging by 90%.

Why? Because "his people can't afford more."

I see myself three years ago in every sentence he writes.

The question: Can I teach him to charge his worth when I'm still terrified of charging mine?

Can you teach what you're still learning?

Or is teaching while learning the only honest way to teach?

**The pattern I'm seeing:**

There's a generation of African developers behind me—talented, brilliant, capable—who've been taught:
- "Don't be greedy"
- "Help your people"
- "Give back to the community"
- "Knowledge should be free"
- "If you charge too much, you're exploiting your own"

All of which sounds noble until you realize it means: work for free, burn out, quit building, and let foreign companies dominate your market while extracting value.

It's not generosity. It's colonialism wearing the mask of Ubuntu.

**What Marcus said:**

"When you teach someone to undercharge, you're not teaching humility. You're teaching them that their work has no value. And when they internalize that, they stop creating. You don't help your community by destroying its builders."

**Memento Mori:**

If I don't teach Thabo, who will?

If I don't break this pattern, it continues for another generation.

If I wait until I'm "ready" to teach, I'll never teach.

**Decision:**

I'm going to meet him. In person. In Soweto.

Not as someone who has it all figured out. As someone who's six weeks ahead on the same journey.

That might be the only qualification that matters.

*End Journal*

### PART 1: THE EMAIL FROM SOWETO

The email arrived Tuesday night at 11:47 PM. Moteka was packing boxes—books, monitors, cables, the accumulated debris of three years in hiding—when his laptop chimed.

```
From: Thabo Ndlovu <thabo.dev@gmail.com>
To: Moteka Tlhoro
Subject: Mentorship Request (I know you probably don't do this)

Mr. Tlhoro,

I hope this email finds you well. My name is Thabo Ndlovu. I'm a developer in Soweto, Johannesburg. I build software for small retailers—inventory management, point-of-sale systems, basic analytics.

Dr. Naledi Khumalo gave me your email. She said you might be willing to talk to me about pricing and business strategy. I know you're busy. I know this is probably presumptuous. But I'm desperate.

I built a system that's helping 23 small shops in Soweto manage their inventory better. It saves them hours of manual work every week. It prevents stockouts. It reduces theft. It's genuinely useful.

I charge R500/month ($27 USD).

Everyone tells me this is too expensive. "Thabo, these are small businesses. Poor communities. You should charge less. Or give it away for free. You're from Soweto—you should help your people."

So I've been considering making it free with ads. Or dropping to R200/month. Or just open-sourcing it and getting a corporate job.

But then I saw your interview with the African Tech Review (congratulations on the Stuttgart contract, by the way). You said: "The narrative that African developers should undercharge to be 'accessible' is neocolonial thinking disguised as Ubuntu. Value is value regardless of geography."

That quote has been stuck in my head for three weeks.

So here's my question: Am I being greedy for wanting to charge what my work is worth? Or am I being responsible?

I don't expect you to solve this for me. But if you have 30 minutes to talk, I'd be grateful.

Either way, thank you for saying publicly what many of us think privately.

Best,
Thabo Ndlovu
```

Moteka read it three times.

Then he looked around his workshop. Boxes everywhere. The squeaky chair. The whiteboard with equations he'd been meaning to erase. The space where he'd hidden for three years while asking himself the exact question Thabo was asking now:

*What gives me the right to charge what I'm worth?*

The four voices immediately started arguing:

*Frightened Voice: Don't respond. You barely have this figured out yourself. You'll give him bad advice. He'll fail and blame you.*

*Confident Voice: Or you'll give him exactly what you needed three years ago—permission to value his work. Someone to say: you're not greedy, you're accurate.*

*Rational Voice: You're six weeks into emergence. You just joined the Circle. You're still learning. But that might make you the perfect teacher—you remember what it's like to be terrified.*

*Deepest Voice: This is why you emerged. Not just for yourself. For the ones coming up behind you.*

Moteka opened a reply:

```
Thabo,

Yes. Let's talk.

But not over video call. In person. I want to see your system. I want to meet your clients. I want to understand the full context before offering advice.

I'm in Warrenton now (packing to move to Cape Town). Can you meet me in Johannesburg this Thursday? I'll drive up. We'll spend the day together. You show me everything. Then we'll talk strategy.

One condition: Be prepared to hear things that will make you uncomfortable. I'm not going to tell you what you want to hear. I'm going to tell you what you need to hear. If you're okay with that, we're on.

Also: R500/month for inventory management is criminal underpricing. But we'll get to that.

- Moteka

P.S. You're not greedy. You're undertrained in business. Different problem.
```

He hit send before the Frightened Voice could talk him out of it.

### PART 2: THE DRIVE TO SOWETO (THURSDAY)

Moteka left Warrenton at 5:30 AM Thursday morning. The N1 highway north to Johannesburg, then into the city's chaotic heart, then southwest to Soweto.

Soweto. Where Thabo lived. Where millions lived. The township that had produced Mandela, Tutu, generations of activists, artists, builders.

Also: where "community responsibility" became a cage for talented people who were taught their gifts belonged to everyone except themselves.

*Confident Voice: You're going to tell him he should charge more. In Soweto. Where poverty is real. Where community matters. Where Ubuntu isn't abstract—it's survival.*

*Frightened Voice: And he'll think you're an out-of-touch technocrat who doesn't understand his context. Or worse—he'll think you're right and try to charge more and get destroyed by his community.*

*Rational Voice: Context matters. But undercharging doesn't help poor communities. It just ensures the people who could help them burn out and quit.*

*Deepest Voice: Teach him what the Circle taught you: sustainable value creation serves more people than unsustainable charity.*

Moteka pulled up to the address Thabo had sent: a small house in Orlando East, one of Soweto's older sections. Modest but well-maintained. A small garden. A satellite dish.

Thabo was waiting outside. Young—early twenties probably. Tall, thin, wearing a "CODE IS POETRY" t-shirt and jeans. Nervous energy radiating from him.

"Mr. Tlhoro?" he called as Moteka got out of the car.

"Moteka is fine. You're Thabo?"

They shook hands. Thabo's grip was firm but his hand was trembling slightly.

"Thank you for coming all this way," Thabo said. "I wasn't sure you'd actually—I mean, I know you're busy with—"

"I was you," Moteka interrupted. "Three years ago. Different details, same pattern. So yes, I came. Now show me what you've built."

### PART 3: THE SYSTEM (BRILLIANT AND UNDERPRICED)

Thabo's "office" was his bedroom. Laptop on a desk. Poster of Grace Hopper on the wall. Whiteboard covered in system architecture diagrams. Textbooks stacked in the corner—Python, SQL, business strategy.

"I built it in Django," Thabo said, pulling up the application. "Basic CRUD operations, but optimized for township retailers. Most of them don't have great internet, so I made it work offline with background sync. Battery-efficient for old phones. Simple UI because many owners are older and not tech-savvy."

He clicked through screens:
- Inventory tracking (by product, supplier, location)
- Sales analytics (daily, weekly, monthly trends)
- Automatic reorder suggestions (based on sales patterns)
- Theft detection (variance analysis between expected and actual stock)
- Multi-device sync (owner can check inventory from phone while suppliers check from tablet)

Moteka watched in silence. The code was clean. The UX was thoughtful. The features were practical, not flashy.

This was professional-grade work.

"How long did this take you to build?" Moteka asked.

"Six months. Full-time. I quit my job at a call center to do it."

"So you invested six months of your life, living on savings, to build this?"

"Yes."

"And now you charge R500/month. How many clients?"

"Twenty-three."

"That's R11,500/month total revenue."

"Yes."

"How much does it cost you to maintain?"

"Server costs, R2,000/month. Phone support time, maybe R3,000 in opportunity cost. Updates and bug fixes, another R2,000. So around R7,000 total."

"Leaving you R4,500/month profit. After six months of development."

Thabo nodded, looking uncomfortable.

"Thabo. A call center job would pay you what? R8,000/month?"

"About that."

"So you invested six months, built something valuable, and now you make half what you made at the call center. While serving 23 clients. Does that math make sense to you?"

"When you say it like that, it sounds stupid."

"It is stupid. But it's not your fault. You've been taught that charging fairly is greedy. Let me guess—people in your community said: 'Thabo, don't charge too much. These are your people. Help them.'"

Thabo laughed bitterly. "Every single person. My mother. My pastor. My friends. 'Thabo has skills, he should help the community, not extract from it.'"

"And what happens when you burn out from working for less than call center wages?"

"I... I go back to the call center. And stop building."

"Exactly. And then who helps the retailers?"

Silence.

"That's the trap," Moteka said. "It's disguised as Ubuntu. As community responsibility. As not being greedy. But what it actually does is destroy the builders. And then the community has no builders. And then foreign companies come in and charge 10x what you were charging. And everyone loses."

Thabo sat down heavily on his bed.

"So what do I do? If I raise my prices, I lose clients. If I don't, I starve. Either way, I fail."

"Maybe. Or maybe you're asking the wrong question."

"What's the right question?"

Moteka pulled up the other chair in the room. Sat down. This was the moment. The teaching. The thing he'd never done before.

*Can you teach what you're still learning?*

*About to find out.*

---

### PART 4: THE SOCRATIC DIALOGUE (ON VALUE AND COMMUNITY)

"Let me ask you something," Moteka said. "What does your system do for a retailer?"

"Helps them manage inventory."

"More specifically."

"It... it tracks what they have, what they're selling, what they need to reorder. It prevents them from running out of popular items. It alerts them to theft. It shows them sales patterns so they can plan better."

"And how much time does it save them per week?"

Thabo thought. "Maybe... five hours? They used to do manual counts, manual logs. Now it's automatic."

"Five hours per week. Twenty hours per month. How much is a retailer's time worth per hour?"

"I don't know. R50?"

"Let's say R100 to be conservative. That's R2,000 per month in time saved. Plus prevented stockouts—how much revenue does a typical client lose from stockouts?"

"Hard to say. Maybe R1,000/month in missed sales?"

"Plus theft detection. How much theft does your system prevent?"

"One client told me it caught R3,000/month in employee theft she didn't know was happening."

Moteka pulled out his phone and opened the calculator.

"Time saved: R2,000
Stockout prevention: R1,000
Theft detection: R3,000
Total value per month: R6,000"

He showed Thabo the screen.

"You're charging R500 for something that creates R6,000 of value. You're giving away R5,500 of value every month. Per client. Times 23 clients, you're giving away R126,500 of value per month."

Thabo stared at the numbers.

"But they can't afford R6,000/month."

"I didn't say charge R6,000. I said recognize that's the value you create. The question isn't 'what can they afford?' The question is: 'what's a fair exchange?'"

"What's the difference?"

"'What can they afford' makes you responsible for their entire financial situation. You're not. You're a vendor, not a charity. 'Fair exchange' means: I create X value, I charge Y price where Y is significantly less than X so you win, but Y is enough that I stay in business."

"So what should I charge?"

"What would make this sustainable for you? Enough to live on, maintain the system, continue developing it, and maybe hire help eventually?"

Thabo calculated mentally. "R30,000/month revenue? That would let me live, cover costs, and save a bit."

"Thirty thousand divided by 23 clients is R1,300 per client per month."

"That's more than double what I charge now."

"It's also less than one-quarter of the value you create. They get R6,000 of value for R1,300. That's a 4.6x return. They're still winning massively."

"But will they pay it?"

"Some will. Some won't. That's fine."

"If I lose clients, I make less money."

"Maybe. Or maybe you lose 10 clients and keep 13. Thirteen times R1,300 is R16,900. Which is more than 23 times R500."

Thabo blinked. "Wait, that's... you're right."

"Plus, you're not spending support time on clients who don't value your work. So you can use that time to acquire better clients. Or build new features. Or teach others."

"But what about the ones who can't afford R1,300? They go back to manual inventory?"

"Or they find a cheaper solution. Or they share the cost with other retailers. Or they figure it out. But Thabo—and this is important—*their financial situation is not your responsibility.*"

The words landed hard. Thabo looked uncomfortable.

"That sounds... cold."

"Does it? Let me ask you: when you buy electricity from Eskom, does the power company give you a discount because you're from Soweto?"

"No."

"When you buy airtime from Vodacom, do they charge you half-price because you're helping your community?"

"No."

"When you went to the call center, did they pay you more because you have a family to support?"

"No. They paid market rate."

"Exactly. Because in all those cases, the companies have a responsibility to provide value at a fair price—but they don't have a responsibility to manage your finances. That's your job. So why should you have a different standard?"

"Because... because I'm from here. I'm part of the community."

"And that's exactly the trap. You're from here, so you're expected to sacrifice. But Eskom isn't from here. Vodacom isn't from here. So they charge fairly and stay in business. And you charge unfairly and starve. Who wins in that scenario?"

Thabo was quiet for a long time.

"The foreign companies," he said finally.

"The foreign companies. Because when you burn out and quit, who fills the gap? A foreign SaaS company charging R5,000/month. With worse service. With no local context. But they'll stay in business because they charge fairly. And your community will pay it because they have no choice."

"So undercharging doesn't help my community."

"Undercharging destroys your ability to serve your community. The most generous thing you can do is charge enough to stay in business. Because sustainability serves more people than charity."

### PART 5: THE FLASHBACK (MOTEKA'S OWN JOURNEY)

"Let me tell you a story," Moteka said.

He stood and walked to Thabo's window. Looked out at Soweto. Matchbox houses stretching to the horizon. Satellite dishes. Power lines. Life everywhere.

"Ten years ago, I worked for a tech company in Johannesburg. Good salary. R35,000/month. I was their best developer. I knew it. They knew it."

"What happened?"

"I built a system that automated half the company's operations. Saved them millions annually. They gave me a R2,000 bonus and a certificate."

"That's insulting."

"That's employment. You trade time for money. Results don't matter. Face-time does. I could have been mediocre and still made R35,000. I was exceptional and made R35,000. Same outcome."

"So you quit?"

"Eventually. But first, I asked for a raise. Explained the value I'd created. Showed them the ROI. Asked for R50,000/month."

"Did you get it?"

"They offered R38,000. 'The maximum allowed by our compensation band.' I asked what would happen if I left. They said, 'We'll hire someone else for R35,000.'"

Moteka turned back to Thabo.

"That's when I learned the lesson you're learning now: employed people get paid for their time. Builders get paid for their value. And there's a Grand Canyon between those two numbers."

"So you started freelancing?"

"I started freelancing. Charged R2,000/day. Thought that was premium pricing. Was booking 15 days/month. Making R30,000. Less than my salary but I was 'free.'"

"What changed?"

"I met a client who asked me a question. He said: 'Moteka, how long will this project take?' I said, 'Thirty days.' He said, 'And how much will it save me?' I said, 'About R500,000 per year.' He said, 'So you want R60,000 to create R500,000 of value?' I said yes. He said, 'That seems underpriced.'"

Thabo laughed. "He actually told you to charge more?"

"He did. Because he understood something I didn't: when you severely underprice, you look inexperienced. You signal that you don't understand your own value. And that makes clients nervous."

"What did you charge him?"

"I charged him R150,000 for the project. Which was still underpriced, but it was triple my original quote. And you know what happened?"

"He paid it?"

"Without negotiating. Didn't even blink. Because from his perspective, R150,000 for R500,000 of value was still a steal. I was optimizing for his approval. He was optimizing for value. And value-based pricing is always higher than approval-based pricing."

Moteka sat back down.

"That client taught me: the people who push back on your pricing are usually the people who don't understand the value. The people who understand the value are happy to pay. Your job isn't to convince everyone. Your job is to find the people who already get it."

"So you're saying I should raise my price and let clients who can't afford it go?"

"I'm saying you should charge what makes this sustainable. And let clients self-select. The ones who see the value will pay. The ones who don't, won't. And that's fine."

"But I'll lose clients."

"You'll lose the wrong clients. And make room for the right ones."

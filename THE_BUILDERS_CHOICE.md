## PART I: THE MAN WHO DISAPPEARED

---

### CHAPTER 1: The Algorithm at Midnight

The cursor blinked at Moteka Mokoena like an accusation.

3:47 AM. Warrenton, Northern Cape. The kind of silence that only exists in small towns where the nearest city might as well be on another planet. His workshop—a converted garage behind a modest house on a street without streetlights—glowed with the blue-white light of three monitors. The algorithms flowed from his fingers like water from a spring, each line of code a small act of creation that would, in six months, optimize a supply chain in Stuttgart that currently wasted forty-three million rand per quarter.

He paused. Took a sip of cold rooibos tea. Grimaced.

"What are you doing, Moteka?" he asked the empty room.

The question hung in the air like smoke. Not *what* was he coding—that was obvious. He was building a neural network that would predict inventory demand patterns with 94.7% accuracy, up from the 71% that their current system managed on a good day. The German operations director would pay him three million rand for this. Gladly. Probably offer more.

No, the real question was: *Why here? Why alone? Why at 3:47 in the morning?*

He leaned back in his chair, the kind of cheap office chair that squeaked when you moved, and looked at his reflection in the darkened window. A thirty-four-year-old man with eyes that hadn't slept properly in years, wearing a t-shirt that said "I HAVE A BOOLEAN BUT YOU WOULDN'T GET IT" in faded letters. His idea of humor. His sister called it "painful." He'd told her that was redundant—all humor was painful, that was the point.

"I am aware," he said to his reflection, speaking the words like an incantation, "of what I ought to be."

Neville Goddard would approve of that sentence. Consciousness is the only reality. What you are aware of, you become. Moteka had read *The Power of Awareness* seventeen times. He'd highlighted it, annotated it, argued with it in the margins. He knew the truth of it the way you know the truth of gravity—by living it, by falling and falling and falling until you learned to fly.

But here's the thing about awareness: knowing what you should be and *being* it are separated by a gulf as wide as the Karoo sky above him.

"So," he continued, addressing his reflection with the formal courtesy he rarely showed to actual people, "if I am aware that I am a builder—and I am—and if I am aware that I have created systems that save lives, that multiply efficiency, that turn chaos into order—and I have—then why am I sitting alone in Warrenton at..." he glanced at the monitor, "...3:49 AM, working for Germans who will never know my name?"

His reflection had no answer. Reflections rarely did. That's why Socrates preferred dialogue partners.

The cursor blinked. Waiting.

Moteka turned back to his code. The algorithm was elegant—he'd designed it based on a modified Transformer architecture, but optimized for time-series data rather than language. He'd written a paper about it once, submitted it to a journal, then withdrawn it before publication. Why? Because the moment he published, someone would replicate it, improve it marginally, and claim credit for the improvement. Better to keep it as a trade secret. Better to let the value speak for itself when the German company's waste dropped by forty million rand in Q4.

Let the results be the argument.

He typed:

```python
class InventoryOracle:
    """
    Predicts demand patterns using temporal attention mechanisms.
    Think of it as teaching the system to notice what humans miss:
    the relationship between weather in Hamburg and bread purchases,
    between football matches and beer sales,
    between school holidays and frozen food demand.
    Patterns invisible to spreadsheets, obvious to neural networks.
    """
```

He paused at his own comment. This was his habit—commenting code not for other programmers, but for himself. Explaining to himself what he was building, why it mattered. A kind of internal Socratic dialogue.

*Why does this pattern matter, Moteka?*

Because humans can't hold 47,000 variables in their heads simultaneously. Neural networks can.

*And why should the German company pay you three million rand for this?*

Because they currently lose forty-three million rand per quarter. I'm saving them thirty-seven million. That's value created where none existed before.

*And if they say it's too expensive?*

Then they don't understand value. And I walk away.

This last thought brought a smile to his face. A small, dry smile. The kind of smile you'd miss if you blinked.

Six months ago, he'd walked away from a mining company that wanted him to build a predictive maintenance system for their equipment. The system would have saved them hundreds of millions. They'd offered him 200,000 rand. He'd quoted them five million. They'd laughed. Called it "outrageous." Asked if he was "trying to rob them."

He'd stood up, closed his laptop, and said: "No, I'm trying to save you a hundred million rand per year. But I can see you'd prefer to lose it."

Three months later, after their third catastrophic equipment failure had cost them ninety million rand in lost production, they'd called back. Offered him seven million. "Can you start immediately?"

He'd said no. Too busy. They'd offered ten million.

He'd taken the contract.

The equipment had run flawlessly for four months now. They'd sent him a bonus. Unsolicited. Another three million. With a note: "We should have listened the first time."

This was the pattern Moteka had discovered: the people who understood value immediately would pay anything. The people who didn't understand value would pay nothing until they understood cost.

His job wasn't to convince anyone. His job was to build.

And yet...

He saved the file, pushed back from the desk, and walked to the window. Warrenton at 4:00 AM looked like every small town looks at 4:00 AM—asleep, dreaming its small-town dreams. When he was younger, he'd wanted to escape this place. University in Johannesburg, then job offers from Cape Town, from London, from California. The world had wanted Moteka Mokoena, the brilliant programmer, the mathematical prodigy, the man who could see patterns in chaos.

He'd turned them all down.

Not because he loved Warrenton—he was largely indifferent to Warrenton. But because he'd realized something that most people never realize: geography is irrelevant to those who build with their minds. His workshop could be in Silicon Valley or Soweto or Stellenbosch or here, in this converted garage in Warrenton, and the work would be exactly the same. The Germans would still pay him. The Americans would still call him. The Japanese robotics firm would still fly him to Tokyo twice a year to consult on their manufacturing systems.

Location was a myth. Value was reality.

But isolation...

Isolation was something else.

"I am aware," he said again, quieter this time, "that I am at war with myself."

This was the truth he'd been avoiding. The cursor-blink truth. The 3:47 AM truth.

He was the best at what he did. Top 0.1% globally. He could name maybe fifty people on Earth who understood AI systems, electrical engineering, and business strategy at his level. Maybe fifty. Out of eight billion.

And yet he sat alone, in Warrenton, taking contracts from people who would never understand him, building systems that would change industries but never bear his name, accumulating wealth that he barely spent, teaching occasional protégés who would learn from him and then leave.

Why?

The question echoed in the workshop.

*Because you're afraid,* a voice answered. Not his voice. The other voice. The one that sounded suspiciously like truth.

Afraid of what?

*Of being seen. Of being known. Of standing in the light and saying, "I am Moteka Mokoena. I am the builder. I made this. I deserve to be paid what it's worth. I deserve your respect, your attention, your awe."*

He turned from the window sharply, as if he could physically turn away from the thought.

The monitors glowed, patient and indifferent. The algorithm waited for him to finish it. In Stuttgart, operations directors slept soundly, unaware that their salvation was being written in Warrenton at 4:03 AM.

Moteka sat back down.

His phone buzzed. A text message. From Dr. Naledi Khumalo.

They'd met at a conference in Johannesburg two years ago. She'd been presenting on AI ethics; he'd been in the audience, anonymous and uninterested in networking. But during the Q&A, someone had asked her, "Shouldn't AI be freely available to everyone? Isn't it unethical to privatize these technologies?"

And Naledi had said something that made Moteka look up from his phone:

"Should the surgeon who saves your life work for free? Should the architect who designs your home give away her blueprints? Why do we demand that technologists impoverish themselves in the name of ethics? The most ethical thing you can do is create value and be compensated for it. That's how you create *more* value."

After the panel, he'd approached her. Said: "You're wrong about one thing."

She'd raised an eyebrow. "Just one?"

"You said it's ethical to be compensated. It's not just ethical. It's necessary. If I work for free, I can help one person. If I charge what I'm worth, I can hire ten people, help a thousand clients, and teach a hundred protégés. Profit scales impact."

She'd smiled. "And who are you?"

"Nobody important."

"I doubt that."

They'd exchanged numbers. Now she texted him occasionally. Usually questions about his work. Sometimes just checking if he was still alive. He suspected she was the only person on Earth who might understand what it was like to be him.

The text said: *Can't sleep either? Or just working?*

He typed back: *What's the difference?*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

*There isn't one. Not for you. That's the problem.*

He stared at the message.

She was right, of course. She was usually right. That's what made their conversations dangerous. Naledi asked questions that had answers Moteka didn't want to face.

*Do you know what you're avoiding?* he typed.

*No. But you do.*

The cursor blinked.

"I do," he whispered to the empty room.

He was avoiding himself. The self that he knew he should be. The self that Neville Goddard would tell him he already was, if only he'd claim it. The self that didn't hide in Warrenton. The self that didn't take contracts anonymously. The self that didn't disappear into code at 4:00 AM to avoid thinking about the war inside his skull.

He was avoiding Moteka Mokoena.

And somewhere, in the back of his mind, a question formed. It sounded like his voice, but older. Wiser. More certain.

*What would happen if you stopped avoiding?*

He didn't have an answer.

Not yet.

But the question had been asked, and once a question is asked, it demands an answer.

Moteka turned back to his code. The InventoryOracle waited, incomplete. In Stuttgart, forty-three million rand per quarter evaporated into waste and inefficiency. And here, in Warrenton, in a converted garage, one man sat at the intersection of awareness and action, knowing exactly what he should do.

But not yet doing it.

The cursor blinked.

3:47 AM had become 4:17 AM.

He typed:

```python
def transform(self, reality: Current, awareness: Desired) -> Future:
    """
    The algorithm of becoming.
    Input: what you are
    Parameter: what you know you should be
    Output: what you will be

    The only question is: when?
    """
    pass  # TODO: implement
```

He stared at the TODO.

Then he saved the file and shut down his monitors.

Sleep wouldn't come. It never did. But maybe, just maybe, he could sit in the dark and stop running from the one person he'd been avoiding all these years.

Himself.

### CHAPTER 2: The Tycoon's Question

The Mercedes G-Class looked absurd on Moteka's street.

It was 2:00 PM, fourteen hours after Moteka had finally gone to bed, and he was making coffee when he heard the engine—a low, expensive purr that didn't belong in Warrenton. He looked out the kitchen window and watched a vehicle worth more than most houses on his block pull up outside his workshop.

A man emerged. Late fifties, silver hair, khaki shirt that probably cost what most people spent on groceries in a month. He moved with the confidence of someone who'd never been told "no" without eventually getting his way.

Moteka recognized the type immediately. Tycoon. Self-made, probably. The kind who'd built an empire through force of will and couldn't understand why the same tactics didn't work on technology.

He sipped his coffee and waited.

The man knocked on the workshop door. Moteka didn't move. The second knock came louder. The third knock came with a voice:

"Mr. Mokoena? I know you're home. I can see your car."

Moteka looked at his 2015 Toyota Corolla, parked next to the absurd Mercedes. The contrast was almost philosophical.

He opened the door.

"Can I help you?" Moteka asked.

The man extended his hand. "Pieter van der Merwe. I own—"

"Six thousand hectares of wine farms in Stellenbosch, two thousand hectares of wheat in the Western Cape, and a controlling interest in three agricultural distribution companies. Annual revenue approximately 800 million rand. You're considering expansion into macadamia nuts but uncertain about climate risk."

Pieter's hand hung in the air, unshaken. "You've done your research."

"You're rich enough to find my address. I'm curious enough to Google you before answering the door." Moteka still hadn't taken his hand. "What do you want?"

"I need your help."

"You need my services. There's a difference."

Pieter lowered his hand. "I've heard you're... difficult."

"I'm not difficult. I'm expensive. Different problem."

A flash of something crossed Pieter's face—irritation? respect?—before settling back into practiced charm. "May I come in?"

Moteka considered. He had work to do. The German algorithm wasn't finished. But something about this encounter interested him. Maybe it was the Mercedes. Maybe it was the fact that a wine magnate from Stellenbosch had driven six hours to Warrenton to find him. Maybe it was just boredom.

"Five minutes," Moteka said, and stepped aside.

The workshop was exactly what you'd expect: monitors, cables, a whiteboard covered in equations that looked like art if you didn't understand mathematics and like mathematics if you did. Pieter looked around with the expression of a man entering a foreign country.

"No receptionist," Pieter observed.

"No need."

"No office furniture."

"I don't host meetings."

"Then what do you call this?"

"An interruption." Moteka sat in his squeaky office chair and gestured to a folding chair in the corner. "Four minutes."

Pieter sat, looking faintly ridiculous in his expensive clothes on a cheap metal chair. "My farms are failing."

"No, they're not. Your farms produced 47 million rand in profit last year. Your distribution companies produced another 86 million. You're not failing. You're just not growing as fast as you think you should."

"How did you—"

"Public filings, supplier contracts, two interviews you did with Sake24, and basic arithmetic. Three minutes."

Pieter's jaw tightened. "Fine. My *competitors* are growing faster than I am. Last quarter, Botha Farms—my main rival—somehow reduced their waste by 40%. Forty percent. Their irrigation costs dropped. Their harvest predictions improved. Their yield per hectare is suddenly better than mine for the first time in twenty years. And I've heard..." He paused. "I've heard you might be why."

"I've never worked with Botha Farms."

Relief crossed Pieter's face.

"I worked with three of your other competitors."

The relief disappeared.

"Let me guess," Moteka continued. "You're here to ask me to build you the same systems. Automated irrigation optimization. Predictive harvest algorithms. Supply chain management. Probably throw in some drone surveillance and soil analysis while we're at it."

"Yes. Exactly that."

"Why?"

Pieter blinked. "Why? Because my competitors—"

"No. Why do you want these systems? What do you think they'll do?"

"Make me more efficient. More profitable."

"By how much?"

"I... I don't know exactly. But if Botha reduced waste by 40%—"

"Different farm. Different variables. Different soil composition, different microclimates, different workforce. Botha's 40% means nothing for your operation." Moteka leaned back, the chair squeaking. "So I'll ask again: why do you want these systems?"

Pieter looked like a man unaccustomed to being questioned. "To stay competitive."

"That's not a reason. That's fear."

"Excuse me?"

"You're not here because you want to improve your operation. You're here because you're afraid of falling behind. Fear is a terrible foundation for a business decision."

Pieter stood up. "I didn't drive six hours to be insulted."

"Good. Then sit down and be educated instead." Moteka's voice hadn't changed—still flat, still matter-of-fact. "One minute left. You can spend it walking to your expensive car, or you can spend it learning why you're asking the wrong questions."

A long pause. Then Pieter sat.

"Better," Moteka said. "Now. What is a farm?"

"What?"

"Simple question. What is a farm?"

"Land. Crops. Equipment. Workers."

"Wrong. A farm is a system for converting inputs into outputs. You put in seeds, water, labor, time, and capital. You get out crops. The efficiency of that conversion determines your profit. Yes?"

"Obviously."

"Then tell me: how many variables affect that conversion process on your farms?"

"I... hundreds, I suppose. Weather, soil quality, labor—"

"Seventeen thousand, four hundred, and twelve."

Pieter stared. "That's absurdly specific."

"That's accurate. I estimated based on your hectares, crop diversity, and workforce size. Seventeen thousand variables, all interacting constantly. Temperature, rainfall, soil nitrogen levels, wind patterns, pest populations, worker availability, equipment maintenance schedules, market prices, transport logistics. Shall I continue?"

"No, I see your point."

"Do you? Tell me how many of those variables you currently monitor."

"We have... reports. Data. My farm managers—"

"Fifty. You monitor maybe fifty variables, and you monitor them poorly. Weekly reports. Monthly assessments. All lagging indicators. By the time you know there's a problem, the problem has already cost you money."

Moteka pulled up a monitor and typed rapidly. A graph appeared—harvest yields over five years.

"This is Botha Farms. Notice anything?"

Pieter leaned forward. "The variance decreased. Their yields became more... predictable."

"Exactly. Not necessarily better—though they did improve—but more *consistent*. Know why?"

"Your system."

"No. My system just made it visible. Botha's problem wasn't that they were bad farmers. Their problem was that they were farming blind. They had expertise but no data. Intuition but no precision. They knew *something* was wrong but not *what* or *where* or *when*."

Moteka pulled up another screen—a real-time dashboard covered in numbers, graphs, weather overlays.

"This is what I built them. Not magic. Not artificial intelligence doing the thinking for them. Just visibility. Seventeen thousand variables, monitored in real-time, analyzed for patterns. The system doesn't make decisions. It makes *information*. Then humans decide."

"But the efficiency gains—"

"Came from knowing things they didn't know before. Knowing that the southeast corner of Field 7 has drainage problems that only appear after heavy rain. Knowing that their irrigation schedule was optimized for average temperatures, not actual temperatures. Knowing that their harvest crews were most efficient between 6 AM and 11 AM, not 8 AM to 5 PM like they'd always done."

Moteka turned to face Pieter directly. "Your five minutes are up. But I'll give you one more. Here's the question you should be asking: What don't I know about my own operation that, if I knew it, would change how I operate?"

Pieter was quiet for a moment. "Everything?"

"Closer. But more specifically: you don't know what you don't know. And that ignorance is costing you approximately 90 million rand per year."

"Ninety million?"

"Conservative estimate. Based on the efficiency gaps between your operation and best-in-class farms of similar size. Could be more. Probably is."

Pieter's face had gone pale. "And you could... close that gap?"

"Some of it. Not all. No one can eliminate all inefficiency. But I could make you see it. And once you see it, you can fix it."

"How much?"

"For what I'd build you? Fifteen million rand."

Pieter actually laughed. "That's absurd."

"Is it? I just told you you're losing ninety million per year. I'm offering to save you sixty, seventy million of that. Annually. And you're calling fifteen million absurd?"

"But... that's just for software. For data."

"No. That's for *knowledge*. There's a difference." Moteka stood. "Let me show you something."

He walked to the whiteboard and picked up a marker.

"You said your farms have workers. How many?"

"About 350 permanent staff. Another 200 seasonal."

Moteka wrote the numbers. "And you worry about automation taking their jobs, yes?"

Pieter hesitated. "It's been... mentioned."

"By whom?"

"Union representatives. Local politicians. Journalists asking about 'the future of farm labor.'"

"And what did you tell them?"

"That I'm committed to keeping jobs."

"Are you?"

"Of course."

"Why?"

Pieter looked offended. "Because they're people. They have families. They depend on—"

"Wrong answer." Moteka's voice was sharp. "Try again."

"I... what?"

"You're lying. Not to me—to yourself. You're not committed to keeping jobs out of compassion. You're committed to keeping jobs out of fear. Fear of backlash. Fear of looking like a villain. Fear of unions. But let me ask you something:" Moteka drew a line on the board. "What happens to those 350 workers if your farm goes bankrupt?"

Silence.

"Pieter. Answer the question."

"They'd lose their jobs."

"All of them. And not just your workers—your competitors' workers would absorb the market share. So those 350 people lose their livelihoods so that other companies can profit. That's not compassion. That's waste."

Moteka wrote more numbers.

"Here's what automation actually does. Not to jobs, but to *work*. Your irrigation currently requires twelve workers doing manual valve adjustments, daily schedule coordination, and crisis response when something breaks. Yes?"

"More or less."

"Those twelve workers spend approximately 60% of their time on repetitive tasks that a sensor network could do better. 30% on coordination that automated systems would eliminate. And 10%—only 10%—on problem-solving that actually requires human judgment."

He circled the 10%.

"So here's my question: What happens if you automate the 90% and focus those twelve workers entirely on the 10%?"

"They'd be... more effective?"

"They'd be supervisors. Specialists. Problem-solvers. And you'd need more of them, not fewer."

Pieter frowned. "That doesn't make sense."

"Doesn't it? Right now those twelve workers can manage your current operation. But what if your operation doubled? What if you expanded into those macadamia farms you're considering? Could those same twelve workers manage 12,000 hectares?"

"No."

"But an automated system, supervised by specialists, could. So automation doesn't eliminate jobs—it eliminates the constraints on growth. You don't fire twelve workers. You grow until you need twenty-four."

Moteka sat back down. "This is what people miss about technology. They see efficiency and think 'downsizing.' But efficiency isn't about doing the same thing with fewer people. It's about doing *more things* with the same people. Or better yet, doing things that were previously impossible."

"Like what?"

"Like real-time soil analysis across 6,000 hectares. Like predictive pest modeling. Like optimization of harvest timing down to the hour, not the week. Like micro-climate monitoring that lets you plant crops you thought were impossible in your region." Moteka leaned forward. "Like staying in business while your competitors who refused to adapt go bankrupt and lay off their workers."

Pieter was quiet for a long moment.

"You're saying I'm morally obligated to automate."

"I'm saying you're morally obligated to create value and stay profitable. Automation is just the tool. But yes—if you can save ninety million rand, employ more people, and grow your operation, and you choose not to because you're afraid of looking bad? That's not morality. That's cowardice."

Another long pause.

"Fifteen million," Pieter said quietly.

"Correct."

"And you guarantee the results?"

"No. I guarantee the system. Results depend on what you do with it. But I'll tell you this: every client I've built similar systems for has seen ROI within eighteen months. Most see it in six."

"Why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't. You should verify. Here." Moteka scribbled something on a piece of paper and handed it to Pieter. "Three of my clients in agricultural tech. Call them. Ask them if I'm worth what I charge. Ask them if they'd pay it again. Ask them if they'd pay more."

Pieter looked at the list. His eyes widened. "These are..."

"Your competitors. Yes. I told you. I've worked with three of your competitors. They'll tell you I'm expensive. They'll also tell you they'd have gone bankrupt without me."

Pieter folded the paper carefully and put it in his pocket. Then he looked around the workshop again—the monitors, the whiteboard, the squeaky chair.

"You really work out of a garage in Warrenton."

"Geography is irrelevant."

"You drive a 2015 Corolla."

"Cars are depreciating assets. Bad investment."

"You're wearing a t-shirt with a programming joke on it."

Moteka glanced down at his shirt. Today it said "THERE ARE 10 TYPES OF PEOPLE: THOSE WHO UNDERSTAND BINARY AND THOSE WHO DON'T." He looked back up.

"Your point?"

"My point is you don't look like someone I'd pay fifteen million rand."

"Then you're confusing appearance with value. I can't help you with that. That's a personal problem."

Pieter actually smiled. "You really are difficult."

"No. I'm honest. Everyone else just lies politely."

Pieter stood and extended his hand again. This time, after a moment, Moteka shook it.

"I'll call your references. If they say what I think they'll say, I'll be in touch."

"Don't take too long. I'm selective about clients. And your competitors are still growing faster than you."

Pieter walked to the door, then paused. "Can I ask you something?"

"You've been asking me things for twenty minutes."

"Why did you let me in? You could have sent me away. You don't need the money—obviously, if you're this selective."

Moteka considered the question. "I wanted to see if you'd understand."

"Understand what?"

"The difference between cost and value. Most people don't. They see fifteen million and think 'expensive.' They don't see ninety million and think 'cheap.'" He tilted his head slightly. "You did. Eventually."

"Is that why you only work with certain clients?"

"I only work with people who can learn. The ones who can't learn are the ones who want me to save them without changing anything. Can't be done. Efficiency requires humility. Humility requires admitting you don't know everything." Moteka turned back to his monitor. "You were humble enough to sit back down. That's why I'm still talking to you."

Pieter absorbed this. "I'll call you."

"You'll try to. I don't answer my phone. Email."

"You don't answer your phone?"

"Phones interrupt. Interruptions cost time. Time is the only resource I can't create more of." He started typing. "Email forces people to think about what they're asking. Very clarifying."

"You're the strangest business person I've ever met."

"I'm not a business person. I'm a builder. Business is just the system for exchanging value." The cursor blinked on his screen. "Close the door on your way out. Drafts interfere with equipment cooling."

Pieter left. Through the window, Moteka watched the Mercedes pull away, a ridiculous luxury vehicle disappearing down a dusty Warrenton street.

Then he returned to his code.

But before he started typing, he sat very still and thought about the conversation. About Pieter's question: *Why did you let me in?*

The truth was more complicated than what he'd said.

He'd let Pieter in because he was lonely. Because talking about the work—really talking, not just delivering a system and collecting payment—felt like coming up for air. Because teaching someone to think about value and efficiency and moral obligation felt like building something that would last longer than an algorithm.

Because maybe, just maybe, having dialogue partners was better than having dialogues with yourself at 3:47 AM.

His phone buzzed. A text from Naledi:

*Heard through the grapevine that Pieter van der Merwe is looking for you. Did he find you?*

Moteka typed back: *Yes. Gave him a lecture on the moral obligation to automate.*

*Did he understand?*

*He will. After he calls my references and realizes I was undercharging him.*

*You quoted him fifteen million, didn't you?*

*How did you know?*

*Because you always quote fifteen million for agricultural systems. Then you build them something worth fifty million and act surprised when they want to pay you more.*

Moteka smiled. She really did understand him.

*Is that wrong?*

*Only if you think creating more value than you charge for is wrong. Which, knowing you, you think is a moral failing.*

*It's not a failing. It's insurance. When they realize they underpaid, they come back. And they bring friends.*

*That's the most Moteka sentence I've ever read.*

He didn't respond. Partly because he didn't know what to say, and partly because the German algorithm was waiting.

But also partly because Naledi's observation had landed somewhere uncomfortable.

He did undercharge. On purpose. He told himself it was strategy—and it was—but it was also something else. Something about not wanting to be too visible, too expensive, too proud. Something about hiding in plain sight.

The war with himself, again.

*I am aware,* he thought, *that I am undercharging not from strategy but from fear.*

And once you're aware of something, Neville Goddard would say, you have to decide: will you change it, or will you keep being it?

Moteka turned back to his code.

The cursor blinked.

He typed.

And outside, Warrenton continued its slow afternoon, unaware that in a converted garage, a man was having the first honest conversation he'd had in months.

Even if half of it was with himself.
